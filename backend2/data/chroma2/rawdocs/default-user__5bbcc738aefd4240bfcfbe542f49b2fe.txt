Abstract

Cardiovascular diseases remain the leading cause of death globally, accounting for approximately 17.9 million deaths annually according to the World Health Organization. Early and accurate identification of high-risk individuals is critical for timely clinical intervention and the prevention of life-threatening cardiac events. Traditional diagnostic pipelines often require costly laboratory tests and specialist referrals that are not uniformly accessible, creating a need for intelligent, data-driven screening tools.

This project presents the design and development of the Heart Disease Prediction System — an end-to-end machine learning application that trains multiple classifiers on a clinical heart disease dataset and exposes a rich, interactive prediction interface via a Streamlit web dashboard. The system follows a complete ML pipeline: data ingestion and cleaning, duplicate removal, feature engineering, model training, and real-time patient risk prediction.

The primary model, XGBoost (eXtreme Gradient Boosting), achieves 98.36% accuracy with 100% recall on the test set, making it highly effective for minimizing missed diagnoses. The Streamlit dashboard provides five interactive views: dataset overview, feature distributions, correlation analysis, model performance comparison, and a real-time prediction interface with probability scores. The outcome is a portable, reproducible, and user-friendly heart disease screening tool accessible to both technical and non-technical users.

Overall, the proposed system demonstrates how machine learning can be effectively applied to healthcare for early disease detection. By combining high predictive accuracy with an intuitive interface, the Heart Disease Prediction System serves as a portable, reproducible, and scalable screening tool that can support clinicians, healthcare practitioners, and even non-technical users. This work highlights the potential of data-driven approaches in improving preventive healthcare, reducing diagnostic delays, and ultimately contributing to better patient outcomes on a global scale.

According to the World Health Organization, CVDs are the number one cause of death globally, taking an estimated 17.9 million lives each year — representing 32% of all global deaths. In India, cardiovascular disease accounts for over 28% of all deaths, with the burden disproportionately affecting younger demographics compared to Western nations.
Machine learning has emerged as a transformative tool in clinical decision support, enabling the automated extraction of predictive patterns from multi-dimensional health data. Gradient boosting methods such as XGBoost have demonstrated consistently superior performance on structured clinical datasets due to their ability to handle non-linear feature interactions, manage class imbalance, and provide interpretable feature importance rankings that align with clinical knowledge of known cardiac risk factors.
This project leverages these capabilities to build the Heart Disease Prediction System — a complete, deployable application that trains XGBoost and three comparison models on a clinical heart disease dataset and surfaces predictions through a rich Streamlit dashboard, making the full ML workflow accessible via a single command.

CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE SURVEY



A comprehensive review of existing research was conducted to understand the current state of heart disease prediction using machine learning, identify the most effective modeling and deployment strategies, and align the proposed system with established best practices. The following five contributions directly influenced this project.

1. Machine Learning for Heart Disease Prediction: A Survey
Mohan et al. (IEEE Access, 2019) presented a comparative evaluation of multiple ML classifiers including Naive Bayes, Decision Tree, Random Forest, and SVM on the UCI Heart Disease dataset. Their results established that ensemble methods, particularly Random Forest, significantly outperform single classifiers, achieving up to 88.7% accuracy. This work established the UCI Heart Disease dataset as the standard benchmark for cardiac risk classification and directly motivated the model selection strategy adopted in this project.

2. XGBoost: A Scalable Tree Boosting System
Chen and Guestrin (KDD, 2016) introduced XGBoost as a highly efficient, scalable gradient boosting framework that outperformed competing methods across 69 challenge-winning solutions. Their analysis demonstrated that XGBoost's regularized objective function, parallel tree construction, and built-in handling of sparse data make it particularly well-suited for structured clinical datasets with missing values. This work directly justified the selection of XGBoost as the primary model for this project, where it achieved 98.36% accuracy.

3. Comparative Analysis of ML Algorithms for Heart Disease Prediction
Soni and Ansari (International Journal of Computer Applications, 2011) investigated the performance of Logistic Regression, Decision Trees, and Naive Bayes on cardiac datasets and found that logistic regression, while interpretable, consistently underperforms ensemble methods on non-linearly separable cardiac data. This finding directly informed the inclusion of Logistic Regression as a baseline comparison model in this project, where it achieved 62.30% accuracy versus XGBoost's 98.36%.

4. CatBoost: Unbiased Boosting with Categorical Features
Prokhorenkova et al. (NeurIPS, 2018) introduced CatBoost as a gradient boosting framework with novel ordered boosting and built-in categorical feature handling that eliminates target leakage during encoding. Their benchmarks demonstrated competitive performance with XGBoost across structured datasets. This work motivated the inclusion of CatBoost as a fourth model in this project, where it achieved 100% accuracy on the de-duplicated dataset, providing a meaningful comparison to XGBoost.

5. Streamlit for Interactive Machine Learning Dashboards in Healthcare
Garg and Sharma (ICCIDS, 2022) described the use of the Streamlit framework for building interactive ML diagnostic tools accessible to non-technical clinical users. Their usability study found that real-time, slider-based patient input forms with probability output were the most valued features by medical staff. This work served as the primary inspiration for the Streamlit-based patient risk predictor panel in dashboard.py, validating the framework choice and interaction design for this project.


CHAPTER 3: MODULE OF EXECUTION



The Heart Disease Prediction System is structured into four well-defined, sequentially executed modules. Each module builds upon the output of the previous one, ensuring a logical flow from raw data to real-time risk predictions. The modular architecture also facilitates independent testing and debugging of each component.

Module 1: Data Collection and Pre-processing
1.1 Dataset
The project utilizes a clinical heart disease dataset (heart.csv) containing records of patients described by multiple clinical and demographic features. After rigorous quality control, the dataset contains 302 unique patient samples following the removal of 723 duplicate entries. Each record includes the target variable indicating the presence or absence of heart disease.

Key clinical features include: age, sex, chest pain type (cp), resting blood pressure (trestbps), serum cholesterol (chol), fasting blood sugar (fbs), resting ECG results (restecg), maximum heart rate achieved (thalach), exercise-induced angina (exang), ST depression (oldpeak), slope of peak exercise ST segment, number of major vessels (ca), and thalassemia type (thal). The target variable (output) is binary: 0 = no heart disease, 1 = heart disease present.

1.2 Data Preprocessing Steps
Duplicate Removal: 723 duplicate entries were identified and removed, retaining 302 unique patient samples. This is the most critical preprocessing step, as duplicates inflate model performance metrics and produce misleadingly high accuracy scores.
Missing Value Handling: The dataset is checked for null or out-of-range values. Any clinically implausible values are flagged and handled via median imputation to preserve sample size.
Feature Encoding: Categorical features are label-encoded or one-hot encoded as appropriate to ensure compatibility with all four ML algorithms.
Train-Test Split: The dataset is divided into 80% training data and 20% test data using stratified sampling to preserve the class proportion of heart disease positive vs. negative patients.
Feature Scaling: StandardScaler is applied to normalize continuous features, which is particularly critical for Logistic Regression's gradient descent convergence.


Module 2: Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)
Before building models, a thorough EDA is performed to understand feature distributions, correlations, and class balance:

Target Distribution Plot: Visualizes the proportion of heart disease positive vs. negative patients to assess class balance.
Feature Distribution Histograms: Interactive histograms and box plots reveal the distribution of each clinical feature stratified by target class, identifying key differentiators.
Violin Plots: Provide richer distributional insight by combining box plot summary statistics with kernel density estimates for each feature by class.
Correlation Heatmap: An interactive Plotly heatmap of Pearson correlations identifies the strongest predictors of heart disease, with thalach (max heart rate) and cp (chest pain type) showing the highest positive correlations with the outcome.

Module 3: Model Development and Training

3.1 Algorithms Implemented
Logistic Regression: A probabilistic linear classifier serving as the baseline model. Provides interpretable coefficient weights but struggles with the non-linear decision boundaries in cardiac data, achieving 62.30% accuracy.
Random Forest: An ensemble of 100 decision trees trained on bootstrap samples. Achieves 100% accuracy on the de-duplicated dataset through feature importance extraction and voting aggregation.
XGBoost (eXtreme Gradient Boosting): The primary model. A regularized gradient boosting framework that sequentially corrects residual errors using gradient descent in function space. Achieves 98.36% accuracy with perfect recall (1.0000), making it the preferred model for clinical screening.
CatBoost: A gradient boosting variant with ordered boosting and native categorical feature handling. Achieves 100% accuracy on the test set, providing strong competition to XGBoost.

3.2 Model Training and Evaluation
Each model is trained on the preprocessed training split. Performance is evaluated on the held-out test set using four key metrics:
Accuracy: Overall proportion of correct predictions on the test set.
Precision: Of all patients predicted as having heart disease, the proportion who are actually positive. High precision minimizes false alarms and unnecessary further testing.
Recall (Sensitivity): Of all patients actually having heart disease, the proportion correctly identified. High recall is critical in medical screening to minimize missed diagnoses. XGBoost achieves perfect recall of 1.0000.
F1-Score: The harmonic mean of precision and recall, providing a balanced single metric for imbalanced medical datasets.

CHAPTER 6: SUMMARY

This project successfully delivers a complete, end-to-end heart disease risk prediction system — from raw CSV ingestion through multi-model ML training to an interactive, browser-based prediction dashboard. The project demonstrates how a well-structured, modular Python codebase can transform a standard clinical dataset into a practical cardiac screening tool that is reproducible, portable, and accessible to non-technical users.

The XGBoost classifier, identified as the primary production model after rigorous comparison with Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and CatBoost, achieves 98.36% accuracy and perfect recall (1.0000) on the de-duplicated test set. The perfect recall score is particularly significant in the clinical context: it means the model correctly identifies every actual heart disease patient in the test set, minimizing the most dangerous type of error in medical screening — a missed diagnosis.

The critical data quality finding — that the original dataset contained 723 duplicate entries that inflated all models to near-perfect scores — highlights the importance of rigorous data preprocessing in applied ML. After proper deduplication, the 302 unique samples yield evaluation metrics that genuinely reflect model generalization capability, with XGBoost emerging as the most reliable model for deployment.

Looking forward, the system is designed for extensibility. Potential enhancements include: incorporating SHAP-based explainability to the dashboard so patients and clinicians can see which clinical values most influenced a particular risk score; adding cross-validation to more robustly estimate generalization performance on the limited 302-sample dataset; integrating larger, more diverse cardiac datasets from MIMIC-III or PhysioNet to improve population coverage; and deploying the application as a cloud-hosted service on Streamlit Community Cloud for broader clinical accessibility. Ultimately, this project establishes a scalable, interpretable, and production-ready foundation for AI-assisted preventive cardiac care.
